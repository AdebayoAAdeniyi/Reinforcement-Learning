# Reinforcement Learning from the ground up

Implementations of the core reinforcement learning algorithms, written
against Sutton and Barto's *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*
(2nd edition), with every figure and table reproduced from a single command
and every claim in the write-ups traceable to a number the code produced.

The eight chapters follow the arc of the book: exact dynamic programming on a
known model, then sampling when the model is unknown, then approximation when
the state space is too large to enumerate, then direct policy search. Each
one is a self-contained lesson with a runnable experiment, a figure, and a
discussion of what the result means.

Grew out of the assignments for **CMPUT 655, Reinforcement Learning,
University of Alberta**, rebuilt as a library with tests, reproducible
experiments and documentation.

|---|---|---|

| [02 Dynamic programming](02_dynamic_programming/) | policy iteration, value iteration, generalized policy iteration | 4.3, 4.4, 4.6 |
| [03 Monte Carlo](03_monte_carlo/) | every-visit MC control, off-policy control with weighted importance sampling | 5.1, 5.4, 5.6, 5.7 |
| [04 Temporal difference](04_temporal_difference/) | Q-learning, SARSA, Expected SARSA, and their double-estimator forms | 6.4 to 6.7 |

## Running it

```bash
python -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate
pip install -r requirements.txt
pytest -q                   # 28 tests, about a second
make bellman                # one chapter
make all                    # everything
```

Every script writes its figures to `figures/` and its numbers to `results/`
next to itself, and prints what it wrote. Nothing requires a display, an
absolute path, or a file that is not in the repository or generated by it.

## What the experiments show

Each chapter is built around a question that the numbers can actually settle,
rather than a demonstration that the code runs.

**The Bellman error is a diagnostic, not a score.** In chapter 04,
Q-learning initialised at `-10` has the lowest TD error in the entire sweep,
0.045, and collects no reward at all. SARSA at the same setting has a TD
error forty times larger, the worst Bellman error in the table, and reaches
the optimum. The explanation is in the state-visitation counts, which the
chapter logs: every failing Q-learning run spends more than half its training
steps in a single one of the nine states, and every SARSA run spends about a
fifth. It is an exploration collapse, and it is specific to the `max` in
Q-learning's target.

**On-policy and off-policy control converge to different things, and here it
matters.** Chapter 03 shows that on-policy Monte Carlo control never produces
a policy that reaches the goal on this gridworld, and explains exactly why
with the model's own numbers: `q_pi` of the epsilon-soft policy prefers the
optimal action at the bottom-left corner only while `epsilon` stays below
about 0.2. Above that, walking into a wall is genuinely better under that
policy, because heading for the goal means passing two tiles worth `-10`
while still acting randomly. This is Sutton and Barto section 5.4 made
concrete, and it is why Q-learning in chapter 04, which estimates `q_*`
regardless of how exploratory the behaviour is, solves the same gridworld in
10,000 steps.

**A comparison between algorithms can be a comparison between tolerances.**
In chapter 02, value iteration and generalized policy iteration reach an
*exactly* zero Bellman error on this gridworld, because a deterministic
policy induces an acyclic chain and values propagate backwards in finitely
many sweeps. Policy iteration must evaluate the uniform random policy first,
whose induced chain has cycles, so it converges only geometrically and its
cost grows without bound as the tolerance shrinks: 1517 evaluation steps at
`1e-4`, 3461 at `1e-10`, while the other two do not move at all.

**Decorrelating a batch is what makes many updates safe.** In chapter 06,
drawing a batch of 100 transitions from the whole history instead of taking
the last 100 consecutive ones changes nothing when few updates are done, and
is worth up to 0.80 of the return when a million are. Sorted by update count
the benefit is monotone, which is the signature of overfitting a correlated
window rather than of anything else.

**Variance reduction shows up as step-size tolerance, not as a better final
answer.** In chapter 07 all three REINFORCE baselines solve nine seeds out of
ten at a small step size, so the final return separates nothing. The mean
baseline keeps working at a step size ten times larger than the one at which
the unbaselined version first degrades. That is where the benefit lives.

**Bootstrapping trades variance for bias, and the trade is not always worth
taking.** In chapter 08 the n-step sweep runs monotonically against the usual
expectation: mean return over training is 0.257 at `n=1`, rising to 0.819 at
`n=20` and 0.833 for full Monte Carlo. The gridworld is deterministic with
seven-step episodes, so there is almost no return variance for a critic to
remove, and the critic's early bias is real. The chapter says what conditions
would reverse this.

**An experiment can be designed so that it cannot answer its own question.**
In chapter 05, fitting 100 data points with 100 RBF or tile centres gives
three of the five feature constructions one free parameter per sample. They
interpolate exactly, score a mean squared error of zero on any target, and
the ranking between them is noise. The chapter runs it both ways, at 20
centres and at 100, and shows the difference.

## Engineering

**One definition of everything.** The Bellman error is `sum |T v - v|`,
defined once in `rlkit/bellman.py` and used in chapters 02, 03 and 04, so
curves from different chapters sit on the same axis. The same holds for the
epsilon-greedy policy, the feature constructions and the confidence bands.

**Closed forms where they exist.** `rlkit.bellman.solve_v_exactly` and
`solve_q_exactly` solve the Bellman expectation equation as a linear system.
That is the answer to the chapter 02 question about non-iterative policy
evaluation, it is the reference the tests check the iterative routines
against, and it is what makes the Bellman error cheap enough to log inside a
learning loop.

**Version-robust environment handling.** The gridworld package this builds on
changed its action encoding and renamed two environment ids after the course
ran, and neither change raises an error: an integer policy silently becomes a
different policy. `requirements.txt` pins the exact commit, and on top of
that `rlkit/envs.py` resolves action names and environment ids against
whatever is installed, so policies are written as
`policy_from_names([["DOWN", "RIGHT", "STAY"], ...])` and keep their meaning
either way.

**Reproducibility that is verified rather than assumed.** Every run takes an
explicit `numpy.random.Generator` seeded from its seed argument.
`rlkit.envs.reset_seeded` also seeds the action-space and observation-space
generators, which `env.reset(seed=...)` does not touch, and which drive the
`random_action_prob` used in chapter 06. Two separate processes produce
byte-identical results. `docs/ENGINEERING_NOTES.md` explains how that was
established.

**Tests that check properties, not smoke.** The 28 tests in `tests/` assert
the identities the mathematics requires: `v_pi(s) = sum_a pi(a|s) q_pi(s,a)`
for arbitrary policies, iterative policy evaluation against the closed-form
linear solve, `q_*(goal, STAY) = 1.0` exactly on the one terminal transition,
`sum_a pi(a|s) grad log pi(a|s) = 0` which is what makes a baseline unbiased,
and both log-gradients against numerical differentiation.

**Measurement design.** Several chapters log more than one metric on purpose,
because the obvious one can mislead. Chapter 03 pairs the Bellman error,
whose reference moves as `epsilon` decays, with the exact value of the greedy
policy computed from the model. Chapter 04 pairs the TD error, Bellman error
and expected return, and shows them disagreeing. Chapter 06 reports the TD
error alongside the return precisely because they point in opposite
directions across the sweep.
